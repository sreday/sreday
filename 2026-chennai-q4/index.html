Nix for SRE: The Zero-drift Hermetic infrastructure for Determinism
Abstract

Nix is a crucial technology for a wide range of domains in IT world, one of the important domains is SRE. In this talk I aim to shift the audience's thinking towards SRE and how we improve the workflow. I'll explain the big loopholes in the current industry standards and explain its hidden technical debt in energy, time, cost etc. I'll quickly introduce Nix and explain how it fits in the SRE domain. I'll explain the mental model of proactive reliability vs passive reliability. I'll also explain why determinism is crucial for SRE. The talk will also give an idea of how Nix + AI + SRE is way better than just using AI + SRE. In short: Determinism is more important than one might ever think which traditional approaches try their best, but nothing ever came close to what Nix does in the history of tech. Key takeaways:

  • What typical and AI first SRE lacks
  • How Nix makes infra easy to predict and gives true determinism
  • How Nix provides sovereignty for SRE
  • How Nix removes the complexity of DevOps, SRE workflows
  • Why it's better to use Nix + AI instead of using AI for SRE etc
  • Change in mental model of how u think about the whole SDLC process Note: Though the topic and description may sound too technical, it actually isn't. It's just a different way of thinking. So this talk is for everyone from beginners to veterans.

Neel Shah

StackGen
The Rise of Agentic SRE: Humans, Agents, and Reliability
Abstract

From Reactive to Intelligent: Site Reliability Engineering is Moving Towards Autonomy. As organisations deploy increasingly complex and distributed architectures, traditional methods of incident response based on reactive practices fall behind. The need arises for smarter automation which would allow for quicker detection of anomalies, faster context analysis, and timely interventions within defined constraints. In this sense, agentic SRE is emerging as a discipline in which AI agents learn to analyze telemetry data, establish relationships among various system components, and help SREs with triage, diagnostics, and troubleshooting while respecting certain boundaries. In this talk, I will discuss the principles of building a robust and dependable platform for agentic SRE, the key components that constitute the architecture, and the observability layer which provides foundational data. I will also examine the necessary safeguards required to ensure autonomy while at the same time keeping operations secure and auditable. Finally, through examples of practical use cases, such as alert triaging, incident copiloting, root cause analysis, and bounded automated remediation, we will explore how AI can be integrated into operations to improve reliability.

Navin Pai

StackGen
Runbooks for the AI SRE Era
Abstract

Your runbook has two readers at 3AM now. The tired human it was written for, and the agent standing next to them. The human fills every gap with judgement. The agent fills it with action. "Restart the service if it looks unhealthy" was a fine instruction for one of them. For the other it's four unanswered questions and a production change. This talk is what we learnt putting agents in the incident channel and watching them run our runbooks exactly as written, which turned out to be the problem. Three real failure modes: a confidently hallucinated diagnosis, an unsafe action that free reasoning decided was reasonable, and "remove outdated entries" where nobody had ever defined outdated. Every adjective is a landmine. The fix isn't five hundred lines of YAML. It's making six decisions explicit that a senior engineer already makes in their head: trigger, precondition, verify, stop condition, rollback, and where a human must sit. We'll show the same runbook before and after, the existing schemas you should borrow rather than invent, how to climb from read-only to autonomous one rung at a time, and the part that finally fixes staleness: the agent proposing runbook edits as PRs after every incident, with a human still holding the merge button. The runbook isn't dying. It's being promoted from documentation to interface.

Manisha Agrawal

GE Healthcare
Implementing Cost-Aware Observability at Scale
Abstract

As observability adoption grows, telemetry grows with it. At enterprise scale, collecting everything “just in case” gets expensive fast, and the bill is only the visible symptom. Cost and signal quality are the same problem, and the cheapest telemetry is the telemetry you never needed. This session shares practical lessons from operating observability at more than 12 TB of telemetry per day, where small decisions around labels, log verbosity, sampling, ingestion pipelines and retention can have a significant impact on both cost and signal quality. We’ll look at controlling high-cardinality metrics, filtering noisy logs, sampling traces, choosing appropriate retention, and applying controls closer to the source using OpenTelemetry and modern telemetry pipelines. The technical controls are only half the challenge. How do you convince application teams to accept limits on telemetry they believe they might need someday? We’ll look at the conversations, guardrails, and shared ownership model that helped move the discussion from “the platform is restricting us” to “what telemetry actually provides operational value?” Cost-aware observability isn’t about collecting less for the sake of saving money. It’s about collecting intentionally, removing noise, preserving the signals that matter, and continuously balancing visibility, reliability, and cost. All this matters even more as AI agents begin to act on this data. Attendees will leave with practical patterns for building observability systems where every byte has a reason to exist.

Bio

Manisha Agrawal is a seasoned Observability practitioner with deep experience designing and scaling enterprise observability platforms across cloud-native and distributed environments. Her work focuses on telemetry architecture, OpenTelemetry, cost-aware observability, and turning high-volume metrics, logs, and traces into meaningful signals. She is also a co-author of Implementing Enterprise Observability for Success, bringing practical lessons from implementing observability at enterprise scale.

Srinivasan Rangachary

VastPRO Technologies Private Limited
The Distributed Cache Paradox: When Scaling for Performance Makes Everything Slower
Abstract

Distributed caching is often considered a key enabler of scalability and high performance, but what happens when introducing distributed cache consistency turns a 20-second operation into a 3-minute bottleneck? Drawing from real-world experience building a multi-tenant Java platform, this talk explores the hidden performance costs of cache invalidation, synchronization, and inter-node communication in distributed architectures. We will examine how different caching strategies-including JVM-local caches, JMS-based invalidation, centralized Valkey/Redis, and clustered caching frameworks—balance performance, consistency, and operational complexity. Through a practical case study, we will discuss strategies such as batching cache invalidations, reducing messaging overhead, and optimizing bulk operations without compromising data consistency. Attendees will walk away with actionable insights into identifying caching bottlenecks, understanding architectural trade-offs, and designing more reliable, scalable distributed systems.

Rabel Mervin

Betty Blocks
Break It, Then Trace It: Observability for WebAssembly Backends on wasmCloud
Abstract

WebAssembly moved from the browser to the backend - small, sandboxed components that start in milliseconds and run anywhere, including in Kubernetes. But a sandbox is also isolated. how do you see what happened inside, and across several workloads? We start with a request flowing through WebAssembly workloads, Then we turn on OpenTelemetry on webassembly with wasmcloud and break things on purpose. The talk covers: Introduction to WebAssembly and wasmCloud: what a WebAssembly component is, why it's coming to the backend, and how wasmCloud runs it. Why a sandbox is hard to observe: what you can and can't see by default. Turning on OpenTelemetry: what the host gives you for free, what a component reports itself, and how separate workloads connect into one trace. Breaking things on purpose: we introduce real failures, such as slow services and failing calls, and use traces to find exactly where and why they happen. Logs and metrics: log lines attached to the right request, and metrics on a dashboard. Takeaways: a checklist for making WebAssembly services observable from day one.

Bio

Rabel Mervin is a Software Engineering Intern at Betty Blocks and open-source contributor focused on cloud-native infrastructure, WebAssembly, and distributed systems.

Kunal Das

Kimchi.dev
Stop Paying Frontier Prices for Lookup Work
Abstract

AI workloads have landed on the plates of the same teams that already manage compute, storage and network spend, and the default setup is one frontier model for every task. A file lookup costs the same as an architecture decision. The invoice grows, nothing on it says which task needed the big model, and the platform team inherits the bill. This talk shows the alternative as it runs in an open-source coding agent I use daily. Work is split into roles: a planner decomposes tasks, a builder writes code, a reviewer catches mistakes, and lighter models handle exploration and lookup. Each role goes to the cheapest model that clears its quality bar. Routing decisions are explicit, logged and reversible, and cost is attributed per task, per role and per model from the first day - the same discipline you already apply to every other metered resource. The failure modes get equal time: the task types where cheap models cost more in rework than they save in tokens, what falls back when a tier fails or a model becomes unavailable, and where the big model still wins. You leave with a way to segment your own inference workloads and the wiring to prove or disprove the savings on your own numbers. All of it is open source.

Bio

Kunal Das is a Developer Advocate at kimchi.dev, the open-source autonomous coding agent from the team behind CAST AI. He works with developers across APAC on getting real work out of AI coding agents: multi-model routing, predictable token costs, and spend governance that caps bills before they balloon. Before this: 9+ years in DevOps, 30+ conference talks (KubeCon Japan, FOSSASIA, DevFest), CNCF chapters in Kolkata and Mumbai. His sessions run on live terminals, not slideware.
