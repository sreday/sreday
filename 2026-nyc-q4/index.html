Denys Zhak

What AWS Lambda Was Hiding: Runtime Assumptions Exposed by a Migration

Imubit
Moving a Lambda into a service looks straightforward: wrap the handler in an API or a queue consumer and deploy. That’s what we did at first when we migrated seven Python data-ingestion Lambdas to k8s, and the early tests passed, including concurrent deliveries. As we moved more customers over and left the services running for days, code that had worked on Lambda for years started breaking. Liveness probes timed out, pods kept running out of memory during DLQ replay, tmp files collided, connections dropped and credentials expired. I’ve organised the talk around four promises we relied on by using Lambda: blocking was free, every delivery had the place to itself, nothing lived long enough to go bad, and the outside world saw a different you. We relied both on Lambda’s documented behaviour and on what we’d observed while running our application there. Moving to services forced us to revisit how we managed concurrency, connections and credentials once deliveries shared resources and processes lived longer. I’ll use evidence from the migration to explain each promise, including how fixing the blocking calls made the memory problem easier to trigger. I’ll also cover what to test alongside a rollout, including replay, sustained load and credential rotation. I’ll finish with a checklist for identifying what your application depends on its runtime to do, and what you’ll need to account for when that runtime changes.... Read more

Dalton AI
You build it, you run it. Fifteen years of SRE gospel, and almost nobody questions it. The rule was never about writing code. It was about a loop. Whoever wrote it understands it, so whoever wrote it carries the pager. Understanding was the point. The pager was just enforcement. Agents cut that loop. The code still ships. The author is gone. I take the assumption apart, one joint at a time. No human author, so who holds the pager. Nobody who can explain it, so the author cannot answer the 3am question. Review cannot keep up with the output, so your gate is a rubber stamp. Then the fixes that do not work, and why each one folds. Human in the loop. Make the agent better. Keep agent code out of prod. Then the reframe. If writing it no longer means understanding it, reliability has to come from somewhere else. Operability stops being something you hope the author cared about, and becomes something you check for. One real incident carries the argument. A single S3 copy call, a 5 GiB limit nobody checked, a migration that never sealed, and a cluster crash-looping while the log line said migrating objects: . None of it was hard. Nobody could see it. The principle is not dead. The assumption under it is.... Read more

Who Stops the AI Agent at 2 AM?

Guild Systems

Nibbana

Anand Mattah Chenna Kesavalu

Navigate RUM Without Limits: Cutting 80% of Browser Telemetry Noise While Improving Observability

Grainger
Modern observability platforms make it easy to collect browser telemetry. The real challenge is deciding what not to collect. At Grainger, our digital platforms process approximately 1.5 billion requests per day across multiple brands, countries, and customer-facing applications. As our Real User Monitoring (RUM) adoption expanded, we discovered that a significant portion of our telemetry was generated by bots, synthetic monitoring solutions, automated testing platforms, internal tooling, and duplicate browser events. While these events increased observability costs, they provided little value when understanding real customer experiences. This session shares our journey modernizing browser observability during a Datadog RUM migration and transforming RUM from a "collect everything" model into a scalable, business-focused observability platform. Attendees will learn how we: • Identified and filtered bot, synthetic, and automated traffic before it reached our observability platform • Reduced browser event ingestion by nearly 80% (7.81 million to 1.67 million events) while maintaining visibility into real user experiences • Improved session accuracy by addressing split-session behavior, cookie inconsistencies, and telemetry fragmentation • Implemented intelligent filtering across edge, application, and browser layers to reduce noise and improve signal quality • Balanced engineering observability, business analytics, compliance requirements, and platform costs • Established a repeatable framework for determining which browser telemetry delivers operational value and which should be excluded Beyond the technical implementation, this talk explores the broader challenge facing modern SRE and observability teams: collecting the right data rather than simply collecting more data. The result was a cleaner signal, faster incident investigations, reduced observability spend, and greater confidence that our dashboards, alerts, and analytics reflect real customer behavior—not automated traffic. Whether you use Datadog, OpenTelemetry, New Relic, Dynatrace, Elastic, or another observability platform, you'll leave with practical techniques and architectural patterns to navigate RUM without limits.... Manan Chawda & Nikunj Doshi

Paying for the Worst Day: Sizing Standby from RTO

Experian
We keep spare infrastructure running for the worst day of the year. It consumes resources every other day. We wanted to know how much standby capacity we actually need to buy the recovery objective we promise. We built the same Kubernetes service with three disaster recovery strategies: pilot light, warm standby, and hot standby. Then we failed the primary region and measured what happened. The tradeoff was visible on both sides. Keeping more capacity ready reduced recovery time, but increased energy use while waiting for an incident. Keeping less capacity idle saved energy, but made recovery depend on provisioning, autoscaling, cache warming, and downstream capacity exactly when the system was already under stress. We'll compare each strategy using recovery time, p99 latency, failed requests, error budget burn, and workload energy measured during both idle and recovery periods. We will also look at where scaling from a smaller standby stops being fast enough to satisfy the RTO. We'll finish with a practical method for sizing standby capacity from an RTO instead of assuming that more redundancy is always better.... Read more
